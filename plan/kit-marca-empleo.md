### Post 1 · jueves 9 oct · EN · "Three questions"

Three questions I ask before I trust any number.

I'm an economist. I've spent years looking at data that was supposed to mean something. Most of the time, it didn't. Not because the numbers were wrong, but because nobody asked these three questions first.

One. What exactly was measured? "Revenue" in one report is not "revenue" in the next. Definitions hide more than they show.

Two. Who had an incentive for this number to look this way? A metric that someone's bonus depends on is a metric that has already been managed.

Three. What would I expect to see if the story were false? If the data looks the same either way, it is not evidence of anything.

I now build analytical tools with AI, and I apply the same three questions to every dashboard I ship. The code is new. The discipline is not.

Which number in your organisation would fail question two?

### Post 3 · jueves 16 oct · SV · "Lönsam är inte likvid"

Ett företag kan vara lönsamt och ändå gå i konkurs. Det är inte en paradox. Det är skillnaden mellan resultat och kassaflöde.

Resultat: vad du har tjänat på papperet, när fakturan är skickad.
Kassaflöde: vad som faktiskt finns på kontot, när kunden har betalat.

Mellan de två ligger kundens betalningstid, moms som ska redovisas, och löner som ska ut oavsett.

Som nationalekonom är detta den första frågan jag ställer till vilken siffra som helst som kallas "vinst": när blev det pengar?

Vilken siffra i din verksamhet ser bättre ut i resultaträkningen än på kontot?

### Post 4 · martes 21 oct · EN · "The feature I refused to build"

I once built a measurement tool and made one rule for it: it measures, it never advises.

Users describe something in their own words. The system classifies it and shows patterns over time. That's it. No "you should". No "try this".

People asked for advice. It would have been easy to add. I said no.

Here's why, as an economist. The moment a measuring instrument starts recommending, it acquires an incentive. It wants you to follow the advice, because that proves the advice was good. From that moment, you can't trust the measurement anymore.

Good analytics separates the two. Show the data. Let the decision belong to the person.

Have you seen a dashboard that was quietly selling you something?

### Post 5 · jueves 23 oct · EN · "What AI is bad at"

Fifteen months of building software with AI taught me where it fails. Not at code. At knowing what to measure.

The models write clean functions, fix bugs, and scaffold entire apps. Ask them to build a dashboard and you get a dashboard.

Ask them which number on it actually matters, and you get a list of everything.

That's the gap an economist fills. Which metric moves the decision. Which one is noise. Which one someone is gaming. The model doesn't know, because that's not in the code. It's in the incentives.

So the division of labour is simple: AI writes, I decide what's worth writing.

If you're hiring analysts in 2026, that's the skill to test for.

### Post 6 · martes 28 oct · SV · "När måttet blir målet"

När ett mått blir ett mål slutar det vara ett bra mått.

Det kallas Goodharts lag, och alla organisationer upptäcker den förr eller senare.

Mät säljarna på antal samtal: ni får många korta samtal.
Mät supporten på svarstid: ni får snabba svar som inte löser något.
Mät utvecklarna på antal rader kod: ni får mycket kod.

Problemet är inte människorna. Problemet är att måttet skapade ett incitament, och incitament fungerar.

Så innan jag litar på ett nyckeltal frågar jag: vem belönas för att det ser bra ut?

Vilket nyckeltal i din organisation har redan blivit ett mål?

### Post 7 · jueves 30 oct · EN · "Privacy is an economic decision"

I built a tool that processes sensitive data on the device, never on a server. People assumed it was an ethical choice. It was also an economic one.

Every piece of data you collect is a liability you now carry: storage, security, compliance, and the trust you lose if it leaks. Every piece you don't collect is a cost you never pay.

The question an economist asks is not "can we collect this?" but "what does holding it cost us, and what does it buy us?"

Often the answer is: less than you think, and nothing.

Privacy by design is not a constraint on the product. It's a line in the budget.

What data does your organisation hold that it never actually uses?

### Post 8 · martes 4 nov · EN · "My first week as your analyst"

If you hired me as an analyst, this is what I'd do in the first week. No dashboards yet.

Day one: list every number the team reports on. Not the reports. The numbers.
Day two: for each one, find the definition. Written down. If it isn't, that's finding number one.
Day three: for each one, find who's measured by it. That's where the incentives live.
Day four: pick the three numbers that actually move decisions. Ignore the rest for now.
Day five: show the team the three, with their definitions and their incentives, and ask what I got wrong.

Most analytical problems are not data problems. They're definition problems with a dashboard on top.

What would you want your analyst to look at first?
